Why Your Contracting Business Stops Growing

Most contracting businesses stop growing because everything still runs through one person: the owner. Estimates, sales calls, schedules and problem-solving all wait on you, so revenue can’t pass what you can personally handle. The fix is to take yourself out of the middle one task at a time, starting with the one that interrupts you most.

A lot of contractors hit a number and stay there. Maybe it’s $150,000 a year, maybe $1.2 million. The work is good and the homeowners are happy, and the revenue barely moves while the owner gets more tired each season.

Why do good contractors get stuck?

Contractors who can’t do the work wash out early, so they never get far enough to get stuck. Skilled ones get stuck differently. You’re the best estimator and the best closer, and you’re the only one who knows which job is behind and which sub is about to flake. At first, being the person everybody needs feels like winning.

Then the cost shows up. You answer the phone at 5 a.m. because nobody else knows what to say. You take every sales call. Every takeoff lives in your head and the books live in a shoebox. If you’re out sick for two months, the business stops. That is a job, even if it pays well.

What is the difference between your value and the business’s value?

Some of the value in your business exists only while you’re working. Estimating, managing, solving problems and being on site all stop paying the moment you stop. The rest exists without you: written systems that run while you’re away, people who can carry them out, and a track record that shows the business works.

Only the second kind can grow past what one person can carry, and only it can be sold someday. A buyer pays for profit that keeps coming in after the owner leaves. A business that falls apart without you has little to sell.

How much revenue do you make per hour you work?

Divide your annual revenue by the hours you personally put in. Say a business brings in $1.2 million while you work 60 hours a week for 50 weeks, about 3,000 hours. That’s roughly $400 of revenue for every hour of your time.

Now suppose the same revenue runs on written systems and trained people, and you spend about 1,000 hours a year reviewing numbers and making decisions. That’s $1,200 an hour, and the business keeps running if you’re gone for a month. These numbers are an example. Use your own.

Growing past your own hours means getting more revenue out of each hour you work.

Which task should you hand off first?

Most stuck owners ask how to get more done. A better question is which part of the business could run without you.

You don’t have to answer it everywhere at once. Pick the task that interrupts you most often, such as answering new-lead calls, building estimates or chasing invoices. Write down exactly how you do it, hand it to someone (or something) with a checklist, and let it run for a month. Then pick the next one.

FAQ

Why does my contracting business stay the same size every year?

Usually because estimating, selling, scheduling and problem-solving all run through you, so revenue is capped by your hours. Growth starts when you pull one task at a time out of your head and into a checklist someone else can follow.

What should a contractor delegate first?

The task that interrupts you most often. For many pros that’s answering new-lead calls, building estimates or chasing invoices.

Can a one-person contracting business grow?

Yes, but only as far as your hours allow unless you write down how work gets done and hand pieces of it off. Even one handed-off task, like the phone or invoicing, frees up hours you can spend on sales and estimates.
