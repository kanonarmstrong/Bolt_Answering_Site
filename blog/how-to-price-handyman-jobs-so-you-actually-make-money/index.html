How to Price Handyman Jobs So You Actually Make Money

The most common reason new handymen quit is simple: they do not charge enough. Here is a practical way to work out your rate, hold it when customers push back, and avoid the discounts that quietly drain your income.

Start with what the customer will pay, not what you would pay

It is tempting to price a job by asking what you would pay someone else to do it. That thinking does not matter. What matters is how much your customers are willing to pay to have the work done. That is supply and demand: if people will pay a certain amount for a job done well, you can charge that amount.

Work out your hourly number

Even if you never quote by the hour, everything comes down to an hourly rate, so know yours. Here is the method Alan Lee of The Handyman Journey describes:

  1. Add up all your hard costs: truck, fuel, tires, insurance, tools, software, and your own pay.
  2. Add 20 percent for a break-even cushion.
  3. Add another 20 percent for profit.
  4. Divide by the number of hours you can realistically bill in a month.

Here is an illustration with made-up numbers: $6,000 in monthly costs becomes $8,640 after both 20 percent additions. If you bill about 126 hours a month, that is roughly $69 an hour.

Do not forget efficiency

An eight-hour day is not eight billable hours. You stop for gas, run to the hardware store, answer calls, talk to the client, and take breaks. Expect about 70 to 80 percent efficiency, which means five and a half to six billable hours. Build that into your rate so you are paid for the whole day.

Quote by the job, but know your hourly rate

Keep your hourly rate in your head and not state it to clients. Handymen are split on this. One handyman charges strictly by the hour, saying a flat price tempts you to cut corners if surprises appear. Another, with more than 20 years in the trade, never worked by the hour and gave only finished labor prices, and says he made six figures every year. Pick one, but make sure your price covers the surprises. Quote materials carefully too, since prices change from day to day.

Say no to “future work” discounts

If someone says there will be lots more work if you lower your price today, be careful. Nine times out of ten that work never arrives. Quote the first job at your real price. If you go cheap, the client will expect cheap forever, and you will be known as the cheap handyman.

Let some bids go

One piece of advice: if you are not losing about half your bids because they are too high, you are not charging enough. Experienced handymen add that you should walk away from people who push you to work for nothing, since they tend to nickel and dime you afterward. Higher prices also signal value, while free work is treated as worth little.

A few ideas from working handymen

  • Charge a fee for the first visit, instead of giving free estimates, if you find that estimates eat up your time.
  • Ask for a deposit before starting jobs you are unsure about.
  • Add a surcharge for difficult jobs, such as hauling tools up a hill or working in tight spaces.
  • Look into property management companies, which one handyman says can keep you busy seven days a week.

These are individual experiences, not rules, so test them against your own market.
